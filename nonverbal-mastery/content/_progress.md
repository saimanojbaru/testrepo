## The depth layer (v1.1)

Each chapter has these new parts, shown in the app under the chapter's reading tab:

- **More scenes, read two ways.** Four cases, each with a scene of 200–400 words, the misreading, and a better reading.
- **One scene, read slowly.** A worked reading in 5–7 labelled steps.
- **More ways it goes wrong.** Four failure modes.
- **Questions readers ask.** Six questions with answers.
- **Drill variations.** One to three for every drill.
- **Three extra quiz questions.** At least one is a scenario and one tests a misconception.
- **One or two extra pose pairs.** These bring the total to 504.

Rules kept while writing it:

- **Sources.** The new material reuses the sources each chapter already cites. Where it adds a figure, the figure comes from those sources. No new statistics were introduced.
- **Cases.** They are fictional, and they include women and men of many ages and backgrounds.
- **Ethics.** It follows the same rules as the core: no manipulation or mimicry techniques, no diagnosis (people are pointed to a GP or a support service), safe messaging on suicide consistent with 2.13, non-graphic treatment of assault and injury, safeguarding through the designated lead, and leaving and calling for help when a situation escalates.
- **Checks.** The contract test enforces the depth fields' ranges, the 9,900-word floor per chapter, the 900,000-word total, at least 500 pose pairs, and no hard line breaks inside paragraphs.

## How Parts I and VI were written

- **Hooks first.** For each part, all hooks were written in one pass and judged against the chapter's scope before any chapter was drafted.
- **Depth.** Every chapter follows the full contract, with teach blocks of 240–350 words. There are no stubs.
- **Falsifiability.** Every teach block states what would show it wrong, usually as a test the reader can run.
- **Balanced quizzes.** Every quiz passes the length rules below.
- **No repetition across chapters.** Later chapters call back to earlier ones rather than re-teaching them. 1.6 goes deeper than the cluster step in 1.15 without repeating it. 1.12 covers a room's atmosphere and leaves group mechanics to 6.10. 1.13 covers the universality debate and leaves the method to 6.11 and the map to 5.11.
- **Deception has one chapter.** 1.14 is the only chapter on lying. 1.2 explicitly leaves lie detection to it. 1.4 mentions the gaze-aversion belief only to point there. 6.7 and 6.12 use interrogation research only as warnings.

## How Part II was written

Part II is the most sensitive part of the book, and it follows extra rules on top of the ones above.

- **Recognition and response, never diagnosis.** Every chapter teaches what can be noticed, which rival explanations to consider, how to ask, and where to find help. No chapter names a condition from a posture.
- **Safe messaging.** 2.13 follows the WHO and IASP media guidance: no details of methods, “died by suicide” rather than “committed”, and stories of help and recovery. It teaches asking directly (Dazzi 2014; Blades 2018), staying, judging urgency, means safety, safety planning (Stanley 2018) and caring contact (Motto & Bostrom 2001). Its central claim is that no body signal reveals suicidal thinking (Franklin 2017), so the body's job is to prompt a question.
- **The gate.** `safetyGate` on 2.13 carries all 12 crisis regions. Its title says plainly what the chapter covers. The crisis screen puts the numbers first, so the gate text refers to “the lines above”. `ScreensTest` renders the gate exactly as `AppNav` builds it (`12_gate_2_13`).
- **Medical rivals first.** Where a sign could be illness (slurred speech, panic-like symptoms, fatigue), the chapter says to rule out medical causes before reading it as emotion.
- **Facts checked on the web while writing.** Sources for 2.13–2.15 are listed under “Needs a human” below. Where a study could not be confirmed, the chapter cites one that could: 2.15 cites Lam, Wong & Sham's 2001 study instead of Lam & Wong 1997.
- **Length.** Part II chapters average about 4,280 words, slightly shorter than Parts I and VI. Clinical topics were kept tight rather than padded.

## How Part III was written

- **The hard line held.** 3.9 (attraction) and 3.10 (consent) teach reading the body for no and asking for yes. Neither offers techniques for raising interest or getting past hesitation; the ethics statement rules them out. 3.10 lists the RAINN (US, 800-656-4673) and Rape Crisis (England and Wales, 0808 500 2222) support lines.
- **Safety-critical advice is explicit.** 3.13 gives the never-shake rule and the ICON routine (put the baby down safely, step away, come back). 3.14 teaches the PAINAD signs of pain in people who cannot report it, framed as signs to report, not a diagnosis.
- **Cautious where the evidence is thin.** The up-nod and down-nod are presented as folk knowledge, the meaning of back slaps as interpretation, and Men's Sheds and grief rituals as promising but mixed evidence. Facts that could not be confirmed (Lam & Wong 1997, Nagy's “32 nations”, Gottman's “5 to 1”, the contempt-and-illness claim, Horne's first name) were left out.
- **New figures first.** Fatherhood and caregiving needed poses the engine could not draw, so the engine gained lifted and lying companions and a bed before those chapters were written (see the pose library below).
- **Length.** Part III chapters average about 3,980 words, shorter than Parts I and VI (about 4,500) and Part II (about 4,280). Every chapter meets the contract, but the drift is worth watching (see the page target below).

## How Part IV was written

- **No tells, no tricks.** 4.10 keeps to 1.14: there are no reliable bluff tells. It reports the evidence on strategic mimicry (Maddux 2008) but treats deliberate copying as manufacturing a signal, and teaches attention as the honest route to the same trust. Faked anger is shown to backfire (Côté 2013). No chapter teaches a technique for steering someone against their interests.
- **The burden on the reader, not the read.** 4.8 places the bias in the evaluator (Brescoll & Uhlmann; Williams & Tiedens) and gives readers checks to run on themselves, rather than advising women to change. 4.11 gives the same treatment to interview nerves, autistic candidates and gaze across cultures.
- **Weak or contested findings are labelled.** The phone-on-the-table effect is reported with the replications that failed (Przybylski & Weinstein 2013; Linares & Sellier 2021). The fear of public speaking is reported with how the question was asked (Dwyer & Davidson 2012). Byron's negativity effect is described as a proposal. The jury studies are described cautiously because their abstracts were not available.
- **One source left out on purpose.** The plan for 4.14 named Casciaro, Gino & Kouchaki (2014) on why networking feels dirty. Harvard Business School has told the journal about data concerns in a study in that paper, so 4.14 does not use it. Exact figures that could not be confirmed (Kruger's 78% and 56%, Maddux's 67% and 12.5%) were left out, and claims were written without them.
- **Pose meanings kept consistent.** The new `p_pro_video_lean_in` is the "too close" example in 4.1 and 4.2, so later chapters use `p_pro_video_call` for good video posture.
- **Length.** Part IV chapters average about 4,100 words. The first five ran shorter (3,780–3,990); the last ten returned to 4,150–4,310 after teach blocks were aimed at 260–300 words.

## How Part V was written

- **About readers, not about groups.** Every chapter in Part V is about how bodies are read, and misread, by people who do not share the reader's background: neurodivergent, queer, Black, working-class, disabled, older, larger or smaller, from another culture or faith. The burden is placed on the reader's assumptions, not on the person being read.
- **Boundaries kept.** Each chapter calls back to the earlier chapters listed under "Boundaries" below rather than re-teaching them. 5.11 is the map; 1.13 is the evidence; 6.11 is the method.
- **No diagnosis.** 5.1–5.3 describe autistic and ADHD experience from the inside and never infer a condition from a posture.
- **Facts checked on the web while writing**, with exact figures left out where they could not be confirmed (for example, Andreyeva 2008's "7% to 12%" and Deloitte 2013's "61%"). Contested findings are labelled: Hausdorff 1999's priming result, Bogart & Matsumoto 2010, Knapen 2018's single set of experiments, and ego depletion (Hagger 2016).
- **New figures first.** Part V needed people the engine could not yet draw, so it gained a floor-sitting preset, a wheelchair, a cane and a long cane, headphones, named cast members (an older man, a heavy-set man, a Sikh man in a turban) and mixed pairs of men and women before the chapters were written.
- **Length.** Part V chapters average about 3,850 words, the shortest part. Every chapter meets the contract.

## Watch links

Every chapter now has a "Watch it happen" section: one to four links to official clips where the chapter's behaviour can be seen, each with a note of what to notice.

- **Official uploads only:** studios (A24, Warner Bros., Sony, Paramount, 20th Century, Apple TV, Netflix, Miramax), licensed clip channels (Movieclips, Rotten Tomatoes Trailers), broadcasters (BBC, Channel 4, CNN, CBS News, ABC News, the Guardian), TED and TEDx, universities, and the charities and organisations that made the material (Samaritans, Scope, RNIB, Time to Change, Thames Valley Police, the UK Men's Sheds Association).
- **Every link was checked.** Each video id was found by searching YouTube and confirmed live through YouTube's oEmbed service. One early web search returned a wrong id, which is why nothing was taken from memory.
- **Notes say what to notice**, without claiming timestamps that could not be checked. Where a clip is a trailer or compilation, the note describes the film or scene rather than a moment.
- **Links leave the app.** The app has no network access and no in-app player. Tapping a link hands it to the phone's browser or YouTube app, so these are the only parts of the book that need a connection.
- **The contract test** requires 1–4 links per chapter on a known video host (youtube.com, youtu.be, ted.com, vimeo.com, bbc.co.uk, pbs.org), with a notice of 8–70 words.
- **Links rot.** Official channels sometimes remove clips. Re-check every id before each release, for example with an oEmbed request per link.

## The people in the figures

The figures were first drawn as faceless grey mannequins. Readers found them robotic and hard to relate to, so every figure is now drawn as a person:

- **A cast of twelve men** in `People.kt`, varied in skin tone, age (two older men), build, hair, beard and clothes. A pose always shows the same man, chosen from its id. Do/don't pairs pass the same seed, so both panels show one man.
- **Families and care.** A companion smaller than 0.8 scale is the main figure's child, and smaller than 0.45 his baby (same skin and hair, a child's clothes or a one-piece). A companion lying down is an older man.
- **Clothes and body.** The renderer dresses the same solved skeleton: shirt, T-shirt, polo or jumper, with long, rolled or short sleeves; trousers; shoes or trainers with soles. Men's trunks are shaped with sloping shoulders and narrower hips, and build widens the waist and belly. Children's heads are larger for their bodies.
- **Faces.** Eyes, brows, nose, mouth and ears are drawn only where that part of the face is turned toward the viewer, so profiles and back views work. Hair is cut away where the face shows, and covers the head from behind. Beards, stubble and glasses come from the cast.
- **No seams.** Inside a "quiet" zone a shape is filled flat, with no edge or shadow, so sleeves join the shirt at the shoulder, shins join thighs at the knee, and seated thighs run into the lap.

Annotations, anchors, views and morphing are unchanged, because the skeleton underneath is unchanged.

## Face plates (added for Part I)

Where the face itself is the subject, chapters use dedicated plates drawn from FACS action units (the standard coding of facial muscle movements), each with a TalkBack description. Each plate is drawn on one man from the cast who is clean-shaven or lightly stubbled, so nothing hides the mouth. On deep skin tones the features are drawn heavier, so they read as clearly as on light skin. There are seven plates:

- `face-smiles`, two smiles;
- `face-brows`, three brows;
- `face-lids`, the lids;
- `face-mouths`, three mouths;
- `face-prototypes`, six posed prototypes;
- `face-micro`, a microexpression time line;
- `face-gaze`, gaze direction.

They are used in 1.2, 1.4, 1.6 and 1.7. Each is drawn from a `FaceSpec` in `FacePlates.kt`, so a new plate is a few lines of code. `DiagramSheetTest` renders every plate in Paper and Ink for review, and the contract test rejects unknown diagram ids.

The figures and the plates now share one cast, so a reader meets the same men in both.

## Quiz rules

The right answer had been visibly the longest option in most early questions, so a reader could pass a quiz by always picking the longest. The contract test now enforces two rules:

1. The right answer may be at most 1.35 times the length of the longest wrong option.
2. The right answer may be the longest option in at most half of a chapter's questions.

It also rejects words broken by a line-wrap hyphen. The stub-marker check now matches whole words only; it had been flagging the surname Todorov.

## Resolved: the page target

Version 1.0 kept to the contract and came to about 840 pages, well short of the brief's 2,000. Version 1.1 resolved this with option 1 from the earlier note: widen the contract. The core contract is unchanged. ## Authoring workflow

1. Create `levels/<category-id>/NN-slug.json`. Copy an existing chapter for the shape.
2. Every teach block needs a `poseId` from `poses.json` and a `disconfirm`. If you need a pose that does not exist, add it to `poses.json` first. The test rejects unknown presets and anchors.
3. Use `*asterisks*` for italics: titles of works, and thoughts or speech quoted in prose. Keep them balanced within a paragraph.
4. Write quiz options of similar length, so that the wrong ones are full, plausible sentences.
5. Run the contract test, and fix every line it prints.
6. Fact-check every named study and number. Cite only real sources, with full details in `furtherStudy`.

The JSON files are the source of truth. Edit them directly.
